🎓 How to use this

1. Copying a photo with a grid

  1. Crop first, on the Photo tab — cut away everything you are not going to draw, and pick a shape that matches your paper: 3:4 tall or 2:3 tall for an upright drawing, 4:3 wide or 3:2 wide for a wide one. Do it now and the drawing will fit the page; leave it and you will find out halfway through that it doesn't. You can always go back to the whole photo — the crop never damages your file.
  2. Pick a grid — 3×3 for a simple shape, 4×4 or 5×5 for a face, more only when you need it. More squares = easier but slower.
  3. Turn on Label the squares, then go to Save and print the matching paper grid onto your drawing paper (draw it lightly in pencil).
  4. Work one square at a time. Don't draw "an eye" — draw the shape you see inside square C3. Where does the line enter the square, where does it leave?
  5. Erase the grid lines at the end.

Tip: tick Square cells so a square on the photo is the same shape as a square on your paper. Then your drawing keeps the photo's proportions.

2. Blocking in with simple shapes

The grid tells you where things sit. Blocking in tells you what shape they are. On the Shapes tab you draw ovals, boxes, triangles and lines straight onto the photo — the head is an egg, the eye socket is a circle, a collar is a wedge.

  1. Find the single biggest shape first and draw it. On a portrait that is the whole head — one oval, tilted the way the head is tilted.
  2. Then the next size down: the jaw, the hair mass, the shoulders. Still no features.
  3. Only then the smaller shapes: eye sockets, nose, mouth. Each one is a simple shape sitting inside a bigger one you already placed.
  4. Stop. Do not draw eyelashes on the photo — the point is to prove the big shapes are right before you spend an hour on detail that sits in the wrong place.

Nothing you draw is stuck. Hit Select & edit and tap a shape: drag the white squares to reshape it, the orange dot to turn it, or the middle to move it — and Delete this shape (or the Delete key) to get rid of just that one. A newly drawn shape is picked for you, so you can draw an oval and immediately tilt it. That matters more than it sounds: almost nothing in life is upright. A dragonfly's wings, a tilted head, a leaning tree — the angle of the shape is usually the thing that makes a drawing look right or wrong.

Flatten it into tones is the other half of this. It squashes the photo into two or three flat greys, which throws away the detail your eye keeps getting distracted by and leaves the big masses of light and dark. Turn it on at 2 tones, and the shapes you should be blocking in are suddenly obvious. Whatever still reads at two tones is the drawing; everything else is decoration.

Both save and print with the photo, so you can put your blocked-in version next to your paper and copy shape by shape.

3. Finding the perspective

  • The horizon is your eye level. Things above it you see the underside of; things below it you see the top of. Drag the horizon line until it feels right.
  • 1-point — you're facing a wall square-on (a corridor, a road going away). One point, everything running away from you aims at it.
  • 2-point — you're looking at a corner (a building from the street). Two points, both on the horizon, usually off the sides of the picture — use Room around photo in the Photo tab to reach them.
  • 3-point — looking up at a tower or down from a height. The third point is far above or below, and the uprights lean toward it.

Drag the round handles. When your guide lines lie along the real edges in the photo, you have found that photo's perspective — that's the shortcut to seeing it by eye later.

4. Grid in perspective

A normal grid is flat, so it can't tell you how much things shrink with distance. The Plane grid tab lays a grid onto a surface inside the photo — floor, table, wall — and splits it into real perspective squares. Watch how quickly the rows squash together as they go back: that squashing is what makes a drawing look deep. It also extends that surface's edges to show you where the vanishing points and horizon actually are.

5. Colour

The Colour tab is a painter's wheel, not a screen one: red, yellow and blue are the parents of everything else. Round the wheel is the colour; inwards is its strength — pure, strong, medium, weak.

  • Opposites sit across the wheel — red↔green, blue↔orange, yellow↔violet. Side by side they make each other louder. Mixed together they cancel into grey-brown. Same pair, opposite results: that one fact does most of the work in a painting.
  • There are two ways to weaken a colour. Water or white makes it paler — reach for white too often and everything goes chalky and pastel. A touch of the opposite colour takes the loudness out without making it paler, which is how you get the quiet, believable colours you actually see. Switch between the two and watch the inner rings change.
  • Mix two colours to see where the mix lands and in what ratio. Neighbours on the wheel mix cleanly; colours far apart go muddy.
  • Common colours — a list of the ones you actually reach for (skin, grass, sky, brown, terracotta, slate) written as parts of red, yellow and blue. Each swatch is made by running its own recipe through the mixer, so what you see is what the recipe gives. Tap one to load it into A and mix on from there.
  • Lift a colour off the photo — tap the photo and it names the nearest colour, how strong it is, and what to mix to get there.

Worth knowing: almost nothing in life is a pure colour. Beginners' work often looks garish because everything went on straight from the tube at full strength. Most of what you are looking at is medium or weak, knocked back with a little of its opposite.

6. Three habits worth keeping

You do not see with your eyes, you see with your memory. Look at a face and your brain recognises it, hands you the label, and stops measuring — so you draw the symbol you know instead of the shape in front of you. All three of these break that recognition and force you to look again.

  • Flip horizontally. Mirror the photo, and your drawing too. Everyone drifts one direction as they draw; flipped, the drift lands on the side you have no memory of putting it on, and it shouts. Don't hunt for mistakes — just look. What's wrong announces itself in the first two seconds.
  • Turn upside down. The strongest version of the same trick: upside down a face stops being a face and becomes a set of shapes and gaps. Hard to draw from at first, brilliant for checking. Do it every twenty minutes or so, not once at the end — the longer an error sits, the more work gets built on top of it.
  • Black & white. Colour is loud and it hides value. Value is what makes a drawing read as solid; in grey you notice the "yellow" wall and the "blue" shadow are the same darkness. Pencil work is pure value, so this is the one that matters most right now.

Squinting hard does something similar for free — it throws away detail and leaves the big shapes, which is usually where the real mistake is hiding.
