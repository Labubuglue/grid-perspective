🎓 How to use this

1. Copying a photo with a grid

  1. Load your photo and pick a grid — 3×3 for a simple shape, 4×4 or 5×5 for a face, more only when you need it. More squares = easier but slower.
  2. Turn on Label the squares, then go to Save and print the matching paper grid onto your drawing paper (draw it lightly in pencil).
  3. Work one square at a time. Don't draw "an eye" — draw the shape you see inside square C3. Where does the line enter the square, where does it leave?
  4. Erase the grid lines at the end.

Tip: tick Square cells so a square on the photo is the same shape as a square on your paper. Then your drawing keeps the photo's proportions.

2. Finding the perspective

  • The horizon is your eye level. Things above it you see the underside of; things below it you see the top of. Drag the horizon line until it feels right.
  • 1-point — you're facing a wall square-on (a corridor, a road going away). One point, everything running away from you aims at it.
  • 2-point — you're looking at a corner (a building from the street). Two points, both on the horizon, usually off the sides of the picture — use Room around photo in the Photo tab to reach them.
  • 3-point — looking up at a tower or down from a height. The third point is far above or below, and the uprights lean toward it.

Drag the round handles. When your guide lines lie along the real edges in the photo, you have found that photo's perspective — that's the shortcut to seeing it by eye later.

3. Grid in perspective

A normal grid is flat, so it can't tell you how much things shrink with distance. The Plane grid tab lays a grid onto a surface inside the photo — floor, table, wall — and splits it into real perspective squares. Watch how quickly the rows squash together as they go back: that squashing is what makes a drawing look deep. It also extends that surface's edges to show you where the vanishing points and horizon actually are.

4. Two habits worth keeping

Flip horizontally — flip the photo (and your drawing) and mistakes in proportion jump out immediately. Black & white — colour hides value; in grey you can see which shapes are actually dark and which are light.
